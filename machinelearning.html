  • Project Statement

      How We Trained Our Model

      To build a functioning recognition model, our group followed the Machine Learning Lifecycle, beginning with the data acquisition. We recognized early on that data quality can make or break a ML model, and as Joy Buolamwini highlights—if the training data is skewed, the resulting "Coded Gaze" will inevitably reflect the biases of its creators. We intentionally gathered a diverse set of 1,069 images, with categories of 272 images of spoons, 349 images of forks, and 448 images of knives.

      Our approach was rooted in the principle that defaults are not neutral. By including non-standard silverware, we challenged the traditional definitions of these tools. For instance, one member contributed photos of wooden spoons and forks, including a two-pronged fork with a unique shape and a knife with a black handle. By including these variations, we aimed to prevent our model from inheriting "Power Shadows," the historical and cultural biases that often define what is considered "standard." We wanted our algorithm to recognize that a fork is still a fork, even if its material or prong count deviates from the Western, metallic norm.

      A significant lesson we learned from the documentary AI vs Human: The Creativity Experiment was that AI often fails when it lacks sufficient information about its audience and context. We experienced this firsthand when discovering that "background matters." A simple shift in the camera's perspective could cause the AI to prioritize the room's features over the silverware itself.

      To mitigate this, our group employed a technical device—background variation. Two members utilized a green backcolor to provide a neutral high-contrast environment, while the other two used various non-green backgrounds to ensure the model could generalize across different settings.

      Finally, our approach was shaped by the limitations of the technology we used. We encountered a "technological standard bias" when attempting to upload images in the HEIC format. The AI platform’s refusal to accept this format forced us to convert everything to PNG or JPG. This file format restriction reminded us that even the digital infrastructure we rely on has built-in preferences that can exclude or "excode" certain data types and by extrapolation, certain data users.

      The Limitations

      While our silverware classification model achieved functional results, the development process revealed critical limitations that mirror the systemic issues discussed in Joy Buolamwini’s Unmasking AI. These limitations highlight that no algorithm is neutral and that technical "accuracy" often masks underlying biases.

      Infrastructure Bias and Technical Standards

      We encountered a form of "infrastructure bias" when the training platform refused to accept HEIC image formats, forcing a conversion to PNG or JPG. As Buolamwini notes, "Defaults are not neutral (Buolamwini 147–148). This limitation reminded us that even the tools we use to build AI inherit the priorities and prejudices of their creators.

      Data Representation and the "Standard Stainless-Steel Dataset"

      Despite our efforts to include wooden utensils and unique shapes, our data collection remained a form of "convenience sampling," limited by our immediate surroundings and cultural perspectives. In this sense, we risked creating a "Standard Stainless-Steel Dataset". Our model inherits "Power Shadows," which are the historical and cultural biases that define what a "standard" fork looks like, potentially leading to the erasure of non-Western or non-traditional tools.

      Goals

      • Deepen our understanding of how machine learning (ML) technology works
      • Understand the importance of knowing one’s audience when designing ML models
      • Dialog with Buolamwini’s book Unmasking AI and her concept of “the coded gaze” in relation to our ML Model

      Deepen our understanding of how machine learning (ML) technology works

      It was one thing to learn about ML technology and another to try to use it. By putting our knowledge to practice, we gained a more comprehensive perspective of the technology that has become more common.

      While this project did not require us to know all of the probabilistic equations behind ML, it did give us a taste of what makes them work well. For example, not only did we read about ML but we also experienced how ML relies heavily on the quality of this training data for its results in our own hands-on ML training. Data quality can make or break a ML model.

      Understand the importance of knowing one’s audience when designing ML models

      The readings assigned in LIS 500 and the experience of the background challenge has in part if not in whole, made us realize the importance of knowing the ML model’s audience. In AI vs Human: The Creativity Experiment, the AI comedy routine may have failed in part because it did not have enough information about the audience. In our case, it would be nice for the model to recognize silverware from around the world, but that goal would simply be too big for the scope of this assignment. Traditional silverware in the U.S. may be totally different in another part of the world, but at some point, we must choose an audience.

      Reflecting on this point, one solution could be to create a unique ML model for each unique audience? The more variables involved in a ML model, the more chance it has for error;Conversely, less variables would increase accuracy.. All one would need is a large number of models trained for particular groups. On the other hand, having a simple interface is much more easy to use. Perhaps there is a way to combine multiple ML models into one interface. Buolamwini provides us with an example of what this could look like in Unmasking AI. She happily reports, “But history has also shown us that alternative systems can be made. In the digital era, the LDK camera series developed by Philips explicitly handled skin tone variation with two chips—one for processing darker tones and another for processing lighter tones” (Buolamwini 44). Perhaps something analogous could happen here with our silverware model if we were to expand its audience.

      A challenge to making a model for everyone is that people disagree. Maybe this is obvious, but one person might consider a particular piece of metal as silverware and another person may not. If this is the case, the ML model needs to recognize the nuance.

      Dialog with Buolamwini’s book Unmasking AI and her concept of “the coded gaze” in relation to our ML Model

      At face value, designing a machine to recognize silverware carries no significant threat to humanity. However, the technology behind the machine drags a weighty load able to crush dreams and deny rights. In Buolamwini’s book Unmasking AI, the reader acquires a reasonable caution regarding the use of technology and the use of artificial intelligence (AI) in particular. What might seem as unharmful at first can turn into something lethal. Buolamwini tugs at readers to question the technology they use and not simply take it at face value. After depicting an example of the harmful effects of technology failure, she narrates, “I used these videos to tee up the idea that computer vision technology was further infiltrating our lives and that the consequences of not being detected by a computer vision system were not just for chuckles but could in fact be grave” (Buolamwini 37). Machine learning underlies both the silverware recognition model and Buolamwini’s “vision technology” example (Buolamwini 37).

      In particular, this assignment has demonstrated in a small way what Buolamwini calls the “coded gaze” (Buolamwini xiii) She defines it as follows: “[T]he coded gaze describes the ways in which the priorities, preferences, and prejudices of those who have the power to shape technology can propagate harm, such as discrimination and erasure. We can encode prejudice into technology even if it is not intentional” (Buolamwini xiii). In my experience with the background challenge, I could see how the test model took on the features of my own context. It helped me see how larger ML models can take on not only features but values and ideologies based on the data they are trained on.

      Conclusion

      Ultimately, the most profound limitation is that AI is merely a pattern recognizer without consciousness or a sense of justice. It lacks the human capacity for empathy and the "will" to question its own labels. As Blaise Pascal famously stated, "man is a thinking reed." This reflects the unique human capacity for wisdom and self-awareness that cannot be replicated by an algorithm. While we can code in more data, we cannot code in a conscience. True judgment requires a human mind that understands not just the data, but the social and historical context behind every "fork" and every "spoon."

      Further Reflections on the Project

      Mari

      As I proceed with this project, I realized that I also had fallen into the trap of what Buolamwini calls the "Coded Gaze". Specifically, I held an unconscious assumption that "silverware" must be metallic, shiny, and made of stainless steel. The irony is that while I use sometimes wooden spoon daily, my focus remained fixed on conventional silverware for the datasets' pictures. Although I attempted to introduce diversity by adding wooden spoons and two-pronged forks, I now recognize that this was only a small step toward addressing a much larger systemic issue. My initial lack of awareness regarding the global variety of utensils was a clear manifestation of a "Power Shadow", I learned firsthand. Even with the added data, our cultural standpoint and what I had uncritically labeled as "common sense". Ultimately, this project has taught me that the most important element against biased AI is not the algorithm, but the human behind it so far. Referencing Blaise Pascal's idea of "man is a thinking reed," I now understand that while we are fragile and prone to error, our unique capacity for wisdom and self-reflection is something AI fundamentally lacks. An AI can replicate a pattern, but it cannot question whether that pattern is just or inclusive.

      William

      Working on this project was revealing in both getting a greater grasp in the understanding of technical AI code-work but also the bias and limitations of AI as well. On a more technical aspect, the AI model struggled, and still struggles to an extent, in parsing out the specific difference between silverware, and I learned in trying to develop the model how much factors like the background or other objects could affect the AI’s discernability. On a less technical side, for our project we chose to focus simply on eating utensils, specifically silverware, which made me realize that we’ve also been encoding a certain bias into it, in that the model only recognizes Western stainless steel forks/spoons/butter knives. Some images do help in variability, such as a wooden spoon, but there is still a notable “coded gaze” involved where the model fails to recognize other types of utensils like chopsticks, soup spoons. If we ever we to expand or continue this project I would want to expand the dataset to reflect a more diverse eating utensil palette.

      Josiah

      Hunter

      This project revealed to me that every aspect of an AI project, no matter how minor, can alter its impact and create downstream bias. Despite choosing a neutral task of teaching a machine to detect silverware, we still encountered decision points that could have created bias. Questions like “Would we focus only on Western utensils?” and “Should we include chopsticks to promote greater cultural inclusivity?” arose early in development. I came into this project wondering how we could connect silverware to Buolamwini’s encounters with coded gaze, but now realize we encountered the same structural forces. When the model rejected HEIC images, it was a quick fix to reupload the photos in a different format. However, this meant extra work had to be performed to participate in the model. When we build around a default user, we risk exclusion or an added administrative burden. This lesson ties to Buolamwini’s argument that defaults are not neutral. I think this is especially important in the public services context, as governments build new digital environments, the design choices may focus on one type of user while marginalizing others.

      I also thought about how model creators apply their life experiences and preferences to even the most neutral projects. Recognizing that three of the project members were white men from the United States, we naturally gravitated to using forks, knives, and spoons to train the model. However, Mari pushed us to include chopsticks. I realized in the moment that I had not considered other cultural cooking or eating utensils, and that we had encoded bias into our training model from the start. We were focused on what was manageable for the assignment rather than creating a holistic set of training data. If we were to deploy this model for public use outside an academic setting, I would want us to include utensils from around the world to indicate whose kitchens and cultures we are representing with our model. This project showed me that even when we build a small project, bias still appears and we must take deliberate action to avoid it in every step of the AI lifecycle, from planning, design, development, and deployment. This project helped me realize this about coded gaze more than reading Unmasking AI would have.
